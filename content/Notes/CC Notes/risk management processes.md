Risk management is the systematic process of identifying, evaluating, and addressing potential threats to an organization's assets and operations. It is not about eliminating all risk, but about managing it to an acceptable level.
### **Risk Management Fundamentals**

- **Risk Priorities:** The process of ranking risks based on their potential impact and likelihood. Organizations focus resources on "High Impact/High Likelihood" risks first to ensure the most dangerous threats are addressed immediately.
- **Risk Tolerance:** The amount of risk an organization is willing to accept to achieve its goals. A startup may have a high risk tolerance (aggressive), while a nuclear power plant or hospital will have a very low risk tolerance (conservative).

### **The Risk Management Process**

- **Risk Identification:** The "brainstorming" phase where you document every possible threat to the organization. This includes physical threats (fire, flood), technical threats (malware, hardware failure), and human threats (insider trading, social engineering).

- **Risk Assessment:** Analyzing the identified risks to determine their significance. This is often calculated using the formula: **$Risk = Asset Value \times Threat \times Vulnerability$**.
    - **Qualitative Assessment:** Ranking risks as "Low, Medium, or High" based on expert opinion.
    - **Quantitative Assessment:** Assigning actual dollar values to risk (e.g., "This server failure will cost us $5,000 per hour").


- **Risk Treatment:** Once a risk is assessed, the organization must choose one of four "treatments":
    - **Mitigation (Reduction):** Implementing controls to lower the risk, such as installing a firewall or requiring MFA.
    - **Transfer (Sharing):** Shifting the risk to a third party, typically by purchasing cyber insurance or outsourcing to a cloud provider.
    - **Avoidance:** Changing plans to eliminate the risk entirely, such as deciding not to launch a service in a high-risk geographic region.
    - **Acceptance:** Doing nothing because the cost of the fix is higher than the potential loss. This is only done for low-priority risks.
